Nachtzauber

Night Magic · Hugo Wolf, Eichendorff-Lieder, No. 8
Poem: Joseph von Eichendorff (1788–1857)

Hörst du nicht die Quellen gehen
Zwischen Stein und Blumen weit
Nach den stillen Waldesseen,
Wo die Marmorbilder stehen
In der schönen Einsamkeit?
Von den Bergen sacht hernieder,
Weckend die uralten Lieder,
Steigt die wunderbare Nacht,
Und die Gründe glänzen wieder,
Wie du's oft im Traum gedacht.
Do you not hear the springs flowing
far off between stones and flowers
to the still forest lakes,
where the marble statues stand
in the lovely solitude?
Softly down from the mountains,
awakening the ancient songs,
the wondrous night descends,
and the valleys gleam again
as you have often imagined in dreams.
Kennst die Blume du, entsprossen
In dem mondbeglänzten Grund?
Aus der Knospe, halb erschlossen,
Junge Glieder blühend sprossen,
Weiße Arme, roter Mund,
Und die Nachtigallen schlagen,
Und rings hebt es an zu klagen,
Ach, vor Liebe todeswund,
Von versunk'nen schönen Tagen –
Komm, o komm zum stillen Grund!
Do you know the flower that has sprung up
in the moonlit valley?
From the bud, half opened,
young limbs spring forth in bloom,
white arms, red mouth,
and the nightingales sing,
and all around a lament begins,
ah, mortally wounded by love,
for lovely days long sunk away –
come, oh come to the quiet valley!
About the song. A siren song of the night: springs, marble statues in the woods, a flower that opens into a girl, and a voice luring the listener down to the quiet valley. 'Gehen', of springs, is to flow; 'schlagen', of nightingales, is to sing; 'Grund' is a valley floor or hollow. The poem comes from Eichendorff's verse tale Julian (1853), where an enticing voice sings it at night; the 1864 and 1883 editions of his works print it among his poems as 'Nachtzauber', with Wolf's words. Wolf repeats 'wie du's oft im Traum gedacht' and the closing 'komm', which we print once.
