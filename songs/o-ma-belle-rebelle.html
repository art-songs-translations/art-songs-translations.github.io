Ô ma belle rebelle

O My Lovely Rebel · Charles Gounod, Mélodie
Poem: Jean-Antoine de Baïf (1532–1589), from L'Amour de Francine

Ô ma belle rebelle!
Las! que tu m'es cruelle,
Ou quand d'un doux souris,
Larron de mes esprits,
Ou quand d'une parole,
Mignardètement molle,
Ou quand d'un regard d'yeux
Fièrement gracieux,
Ou quand d'un petit geste,
Tout divin, tout céleste,
En amoureuse ardeur
Tu plonges tout mon cœur!
O my lovely rebel!
Alas, how cruel you are to me,
whether when, with a sweet smile,
thief of my senses,
or when, with a word
daintily soft,
or when, with a glance of your eyes
proudly gracious,
or when, with a little gesture,
all divine, all heavenly,
into loving ardour
you plunge my whole heart!
Ô ma belle rebelle!
Las! que tu m'es cruelle,
Quand la cuisante ardeur
Qui me brûle le cœur
Fait que je te demande
À sa brûlure grande
Un rafraîchissement
D'un baiser seulement!
O my lovely rebel!
Alas, how cruel you are to me
when the smarting ardour
that burns my heart
makes me ask you,
for its great burning,
for some cooling relief
from just one kiss!
Ô ma belle rebelle!
Las! que tu m'es cruelle,
Quand d'un petit baiser
Tu ne veux m'apaiser!
O my lovely rebel!
Alas, how cruel you are to me
when with one little kiss
you will not soothe me!
Me puissé-je un jour, dure,
Venger de ton injure!
Mon petit maître Amour
Te puisse outrer un jour,
Et pour moi langoureuse
Il te fasse amoureuse,
Comme il m'a langoureux
Pour toi fait amoureux!
Alors par ma vengeance
Tu auras connaissance
Quel mal fait du baiser
Un amant refuser.
May I one day, hard-hearted one,
be avenged for your wrong!
May my little master Love
pierce you through one day,
and, languishing for me,
may he make you love,
as he has made me, languishing,
in love with you!
Then, through my revenge,
you will come to know
what harm it does when a kiss
is refused to a lover.
About the song. A sixteenth-century love song by Baïf, from the third book of his L'Amour de Francine, set by Gounod in modernised spelling: the lover complains that his 'lovely rebel' will not grant him a single kiss, and wishes Love would make her suffer as he does. Gounod leaves out four lines after the third refrain ('Mais par tes fines ruses...') and Baïf's last eight lines, in which the lover imagines giving her the kiss once desire has seized her; he repeats several closing lines (not repeated here). 'Souris' is an old word for a smile; 'outrer' meant to pierce through. The text is read from the OpenScore transcription of an early Choudens print; it agrees word for word with Baïf's poem in modern spelling, apart from Gounod's cuts.
