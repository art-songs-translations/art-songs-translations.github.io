La rançon

The Ransom · Gabriel Fauré, Op. 8, No. 2
Poem: Charles Baudelaire (1821–1867), from Les épaves

L'homme a, pour payer sa rançon,
Deux champs au tuf profond et riche,
Qu'il faut qu'il remue et défriche
Avec le fer de la raison;
Pour obtenir la moindre rose,
Pour extorquer quelques épis,
Des pleurs salés de son front gris
Sans cesse il faut qu'il les arrose.
Man has, to pay his ransom,
two fields of deep, rich subsoil,
which he must dig and clear
with the iron of reason;
to obtain the least rose,
to wring out a few ears of grain,
with the salt tears of his grey brow
he must water them without end.
L'un est l'Art, et l'autre l'Amour.
Pour rendre le juge propice,
Lorsque de la stricte justice
Paraîtra le terrible jour,
Il faudra lui montrer des granges
Pleines de moissons et de fleurs,
Dont les formes et les couleurs
Gagnent le suffrage des Anges.
One is Art, and the other Love.
To make the judge favourable,
when, of strict justice,
the terrible day dawns,
he will have to show him barns
full of harvests and flowers
whose shapes and colours
win the votes of the Angels.
About the song. Baudelaire's 'La rançon' (the ransom), printed in Les épaves (1866) and in the 1868 edition of Les fleurs du mal: to pay his ransom, man must till two fields, Art and Love, and show their harvest to the judge on the day of judgment. Fauré sets the whole poem but sings 'Pleines de moissons et de fleurs' (full of harvests and flowers), where Baudelaire's 'Pleines de moissons, et des fleurs' makes the flowers a second thing to show. The 'tears' of the grey brow are its sweat; 'tuf' is the stony layer beneath the topsoil, here deep and rich.
