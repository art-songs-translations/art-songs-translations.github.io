An die Nachtigall

To the Nightingale · Johannes Brahms, Op. 46, No. 4 (published 1868)
Poem: Ludwig Heinrich Christoph Hölty (1748–1776), as reworked by Johann Heinrich Voss (1751–1826)

Geuß nicht so laut der liebentflammten Lieder
Tonreichen Schall
Vom Blütenast des Apfelbaums hernieder,
O Nachtigall!
Du tönest mir mit deiner süßen Kehle
Die Liebe wach;
Denn schon durchbebt die Tiefen meiner Seele
Dein schmelzend Ach.
Do not pour so loudly, of your love-inflamed songs,
the sound so rich in tone
down from the blossoming branch of the apple tree,
O nightingale!
With your sweet throat you sing
love awake in me;
for already through the depths of my soul
trembles your melting 'ah'.
Dann flieht der Schlaf von neuem dieses Lager,
Ich starre dann
Mit nassem Blick und totenbleich und hager
Den Himmel an.
Fleuch, Nachtigall, in grüne Finsternisse,
Ins Haingesträuch,
Und spend' im Nest der treuen Gattin Küsse;
Entfleuch, entfleuch!
Then sleep flees this bed once more,
and I stare,
with wet eyes, deathly pale and gaunt,
up at the sky.
Flee, nightingale, into the green darkness,
into the thickets of the grove,
and in the nest give kisses to your faithful mate;
flee away, flee away!
About the song. The nightingale's song wakes a love that keeps the poet from sleep, and he sends the bird back to its mate. Brahms set the poem as Johann Heinrich Voss printed it in his 1804 edition of Hölty: Hölty had left only an unfinished draft, 'An eine Nachtigall, die vor meinem Kammerfenster sang', from which Voss made two new eight-line stanzas. 'Geuß' is an old form of 'gieß' (pour); 'fleuch' and 'entfleuch' are old imperatives of 'fliehen' (flee); 'Ach' is the cry 'ah!'. Brahms repeats 'dein schmelzend Ach' and 'entfleuch'.
