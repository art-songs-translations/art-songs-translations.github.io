Spleen (Les roses étaient toutes rouges)

Spleen (The Roses Were All Red) · Claude Debussy, Ariettes oubliées, No. 6 (1885–87)
Poem: Paul Verlaine (1844–1896), from Romances sans paroles

Les roses étaient toutes rouges,
Et les lierres étaient tout noirs.
The roses were all red,
and the ivy was all black.
Chère, pour peu que tu te bouges,
Renaissent tous mes désespoirs.
Dear, at your slightest movement
all my despairs are born again.
Le ciel était trop bleu, trop tendre,
La mer trop verte et l'air trop doux.
The sky was too blue, too tender,
the sea too green and the air too soft.
Je crains toujours, — ce qu'est d'attendre!
Quelque fuite atroce de vous.
I am always afraid — what it is to wait!
of some dreadful flight of yours.
Du houx à la feuille vernie
Et du luisant buis je suis las,
Of the holly with its varnished leaves
and of the shining box I am weary,
Et de la campagne infinie
Et de tout, fors de vous, hélas!
and of the endless countryside,
and of everything, except you, alas!
About the song. From the section 'Aquarelles' (watercolours) of Romances sans paroles, whose poems Verlaine gave English titles; 'spleen' is melancholy. The title adds the first line to tell the song from Fauré's 'Spleen', a setting of 'Il pleure dans mon cœur'. Debussy sets Verlaine's words but puts an exclamation mark after 'ce qu'est d'attendre' (what it is to wait!); without it, as in Verlaine's printing, the line can run on: 'what it is to wait for some dreadful flight of yours'. 'Toujours' may mean 'still' as well as 'always', and 'fuite de vous' is most likely her running away, though it could also mean a flight from her; the lover says 'tu' in the second couplet and 'vous' from the fourth. 'Fors' is old French for 'except'.
